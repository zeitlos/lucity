title: Volumes
description: Persistent disks for services that need to keep state on the filesystem.

The root filesystem of a Lucity service is ephemeral, and should only be used for temporary files you do not mind losing on the next deploy.

A volume is a disk that outlives the container, and the place to put data you want to keep. It is worth knowing that your application might never need one, because volumes come with limitations that the alternatives do not have. Uploads belong in a [bucket](/object-storage) and data belongs in a [database](/postgres), both of which any number of instances can reach at once. Reach for a volume when something insists on a real filesystem. Typical examples are self-hosted software with a data directory, a search index it rebuilds on disk, or a database you are running yourself.

## Creating and mounting

On the project canvas, click **Create** and choose **Volume**. Give it a name between 2 and 16 characters and a size, and it is ready immediately.

A volume on its own does nothing. Open it, and under **Settings** → **Mount**, pick the service and the path to mount it at. The path is where your application expects to find its data, `/var/lib/ghost/content` or `/data` or wherever the software in question insists on. Mounting restarts the service so the disk is there when it starts.

Unmounting detaches the disk and restarts the service again. Your data stays on the volume, so you can mount it somewhere else, or back on the same service, later.

A volume and the service it mounts into must be in the same [environment](/environments).

## One volume, one service

A volume is a single disk, and a disk can only be attached to one machine at a time. That is why Lucity mounts a volume into exactly one service, and why that service is then limited to a single replica: two instances running on two different machines cannot both hold the same disk.

This is the practical side of keeping services stateless. Anything a service writes to a volume belongs to that one instance and to nothing else, so a service with a volume cannot be scaled out or autoscaled. When several instances need to share state, that state belongs in a [database](/postgres), a [key-value store](/redis) or a [bucket](/object-storage), all of which are built to be read and written from many places at once.

## Volumes and rollouts

Without a volume, a deploy overlaps: the new instance starts, passes its health check, and takes over before the old one is removed. A disk cannot be handed over that way, because it is still attached to the instance that is being replaced. A service with a volume mounted therefore stops first and starts again on the new version, which means a short gap on every deploy.

Plan for it the way you would plan a restart. It is usually a few seconds, and it is the price of keeping state on a disk rather than in a database or a bucket.

## Size

Volumes run from 10 GB up to 1 TB. You can add space at any time under **Settings** → **Storage**, and the disk grows in place while the service keeps running.

Storage can never be made smaller. Moving to a smaller disk means creating a second volume, copying the data across, and pointing the service at the new one.

The **Usage** tab charts how much of the volume is actually in use over time, which is what to check before deciding you need more.

## File ownership

For [services](/services) created from an image, the run-as user set under **Settings** → **Run as user** also becomes the owner of the mounted volume. That is usually what you need for an image that runs as a non-root user and expects to write to its own data directory.

## What's not there yet

Volumes are not backed up. There are no snapshots and no retention window, and deleting a volume deletes its contents for good. If a volume holds something you cannot lose, copy it somewhere else on a schedule: a periodic dump into a [bucket](/object-storage) from inside the service is the usual answer, and it produces something you can actually restore from.

Where you have the choice, a [database](/postgres) or a [bucket](/object-storage) is the safer home for anything you care about. Databases get continuous backups with point-in-time recovery, and buckets keep their objects independently of any service.

## Under the hood

A volume is a PersistentVolumeClaim in your environment's namespace, mounted into the service's pod at the path you chose. That is why the constraints look the way they do: a single-writer disk, attached to one pod, expandable but not shrinkable. An [ejected](/eject) project keeps its volumes exactly as they are.
