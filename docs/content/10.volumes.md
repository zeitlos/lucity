## One volume, one service

A volume is a single disk, and a disk can only be attached to one machine at a time. That is why Lucity mounts a volume into exactly one service, and why that service is then limited to a single replica: two instances running on two different machines cannot both hold the same disk.

This is the practical side of keeping services stateless. Anything a service writes to a volume belongs to that one instance and to nothing else, so a service with a volume cannot be scaled out or autoscaled. When several instances need to share state, that state belongs in a [database](/postgres), a [key-value store](/redis) or a [bucket](/object-storage), all of which are built to be read and written from many places at once.

## Volumes and rollouts

Without a volume, a deploy overlaps: the new instance starts, passes its health check, and takes over before the old one is removed. A disk cannot be handed over that way, because it is still attached to the instance that is being replaced. A service with a volume mounted therefore stops first and starts again on the new version, which means a short gap on every deploy.

Plan for it the way you would plan a restart. It is usually a few seconds, and it is the price of keeping state on a disk rather than in a database or a bucket.

## File ownership

For [services](/services) created from an image, the run-as user set under **Settings** → **Run as user** also becomes the owner of the mounted volume. That is usually what you need for an image that runs as a non-root user and expects to write to its own data directory.
